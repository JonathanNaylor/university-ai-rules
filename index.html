Guidance often varies between course handbooks, tutor announcements, and outdated website pages. However, understanding your university's AI policy is crucial, especially as AI adoption among UK students grew from 66% to 92% between 2024 and 2025, leading universities to update their guidelines rapidly (Freeman, 2025).

These models change in line with the type of assessment, level of course, and whether the institution has amended its policy recently. Why "Check Your University's Policy" Is Unhelpful Advice on Its Own

The message that you should consult your university's AI policy is accurate, but incomplete. The problem is that no single policy document covers all possible scenarios and situations. Your university may have a stand-alone AI policy, course guidance, module-level instructions, and teaching frameworks (Fu et al., 2026). That creates four or five documents written on different dates, some of which may contradict each other.

Although there has been a slight drop in the proportion of educators reporting that their institution has an explicit AI policy, universities are not abandoning these guidelines. Rather, this highlights a gap in awareness: several institutions have introduced guidance that remains poorly advertised or buried in obscure website sections (Smit et al., 2025).

The practical consequence is that you cannot rely on a single source. You will need to be familiar with the model your institution uses, where the exceptions lie, and what to do if you are unsure. Furthermore, policies of other universities apply to you if you transfer mid-year or take a joint module delivered by a partner institution. Most traditional academic honesty policies remain vague regarding generative AI tools. Policy clarity directly shapes student behaviour. Where guidelines leave important aspects unaddressed, students are often left confused about what constitutes crossing the line into academic misconduct.

The Three Models Institutions Are Using

UK universities have primarily adopted three models of AI governance across their academic programs.

Model 1: Permissive (with exceptions). The institution allows students to use AI in most assessments, with specific exceptions such as invigilated exams, presentations, and in-class writing tasks. Use is generally acceptable, though disclosure may still be requested. The University of York represents this approach, allowing generative AI as a learning and preparation tool provided final work is not authored by external software.

Model 2: Declarative (allowed with disclosure). The institution permits AI usage provided you declare the tool, explain your purpose, and provide working evidence (such as prompt histories or annotations). Institutions like the University of Stirling, Canterbury Christ Church University, Arts University Plymouth, and Durham University use this approach to foster transparency, critical AI literacy, and trust.

Model 3: Prohibited (allowed only with explicit permission). The institution bans AI use by default unless an instructor gives explicit approval for a specific assignment. Under this model, students must ask for permission prior to using any generative AI tool; otherwise, usage is treated as a breach of academic integrity.

Most institutions do not fit neatly into a single category across all departments. Instead, they combine aspects of Permissive, Declarative, and Prohibited rules depending on course objectives and faculty preferences.

Where Policies Agree—The Small Common Core

There are four universal points across all of the 15 institutions analysed:

  1. Exams and timed assessments prohibit AI use. There is no 'no exception' clause. No institution will allow any use of generative AI in any time-bounded evaluation. When the task requires mental calculation and/or writing, AI is not allowed. This is the strongest statement in UK higher education policy on this issue.
  2. Authorship is non-negotiable. All institutions have a policy against plagiarising or "false authorship," defined as submitting AI-generated text as a student's own. Academic dishonesty involves producing and/or altering, whole or in part, any academic work in any manner that is not approved or acknowledged, either with another student or with external assistance, such as using a computer. While there are different definitions of what constitutes substantially, there is little doubt about the principle. Just because an AI created an essay does not mean it is your own thinking and should be accepted as such.
  3. Use must align with learning outcomes. All institutions use the purpose of the assessment as a criterion for allowing AI use. For all institutions, AI use is connected to the purpose of the assessment (test of understanding, test of writing ability, test of synthesis of research, test of problem solving). When the learning outcome is 'demonstrates independent writing', it is counterproductive to use AI to write it. Using AI as a resource might be acceptable if the learning outcome is "solve a problem using given resources. The same goes for institutions and students, as they should be aligned on the focus of AI for learning.
  4. Institutional policy is a minimum standard and not a maximum. All universities state that individual teachers may be more demanding than institutional policy, but not more careless. If your University allows you to use AI on essays, and your tutor says that they do not, the module policy takes precedence. This means the most restrictive policy applies.

Where They Diverge Most Sharply

Declaration requirements. Some universities do not require disclosure if AI was used within acceptable limits. Others enforce mandatory declaration forms. A third group leaves disclosure to tutor discretion, creating confusion for students transitioning between modules.

Editing and reframing. Policies differ significantly on AI-powered editing and grammar checks. While some universities allow AI to refine clarity, others treat automated restructuring as improper assistance. This grey area accounts for most unintended policy breaches.

Research and brainstorming. Most universities allow AI for idea generation, but definitions of "research" vary. Using AI to summarise literature or generate essay outlines may be encouraged by one tutor but penalised by another within the same institution.

Declaration: What a Declaration Statement Usually Has to Contain

If your school or college mandates a declaration, it is likely to require:

  • The prompts or questions you asked the tool to generate the content (what you said to ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Claude, etc.).
  • Why (brainstorming essay structure, checking grammar, creating initial thoughts, summarising source material)
  • How much of the final work reflects that use (rough percentage or description like "less than 10%," "for the introduction only," "throughout the research phase")
  • How you used the output ( Edited extensively, rejected much of it, used it to build on, incorporated into own thinking)

If you make a declaration, this does not mean that you are apologising or confessing. It is transparent. If they need it, they are not trying to catch you; they are trying to understand how you learn and how you work with tools. The institutions named as policy exemplars (Stirling, Canterbury Christ Church, Arts University Plymouth and Durham) are incorporating this into the design of their assessment process, and declaration is becoming an integral part of the learning process, rather than a compliance commitment (The Academic Papers UK, 2026).

What Detection Tools Are and Are Not Used For (Stated Carefully)

Most UK universities no longer rely on AI detection tools as definitive proof of academic misconduct. Recognising high false-positive rates, institutional focus has shifted toward authentic assessment design and viva discussions.

Several prominent global and UK institutions have disabled automated AI detection features in software like Turnitin. Because detection scores cannot reliably distinguish between human and synthetic text, they cannot serve as sole evidence in disciplinary proceedings.

Institutions that refer to detection tools do so judiciously: They describe the kind of work that the tool is designed to perform, rather than what it can do. Example: "Use of any specific tool is not a definitive indicator of academic dishonesty, but should be used to alert instructors to possible AI-generated material to which they may want to follow up with questions”. This is honest. Current policies are shifting away from how you will be identified and assessed after submission toward designing assessment questions that require your thinking, not your ability to prompt an AI.

In 2025 and 2026, UK higher education shifted focus from prohibition toward ethical integration, guidance, and transparent disclosure frameworks.

Transfer Students and Cross-Institutional Module Taking

If you transfer universities or take joint modules across institutions, remember that the host university’s AI policy applies to that specific coursework. Never assume rules from your home department carry over automatically.

Moving between institutions with different models can expose you to unexpected risks. For instance, transferring from a permissive environment to a declarative or restrictive one requires immediate adjustment of your study habits.

Always verify which institution's regulations govern your specific module before starting an assignment. When in doubt, ask your module leader directly for written clarification.

What to Do If You Are Unsure About a Specific Assessment

First, review your module handbook and check specific assignment briefs. If you need structural guidance, seeking assignment help through institutional tutoring programs or writing centres can assist in organising your work properly without breaching academic policy.

Second, check your university's official academic integrity portal or student support pages for overarching AI policy guidelines.

Third, contact your tutor directly if instructions remain unclear. Ask precise questions about permitted tools for specific assignment tasks.

Fourth, consult your programme leader or student advice services if module guidelines seem contradictory. Avoid guessing or making assumptions.

Do not assume that what was acceptable for one module or academic year applies universally to all your current assessments.

How to Read Your Own Policy in Five Minutes

When reviewing your university's AI policy, focus on these five key sections to ensure complete compliance:

  1. Prohibited categories: Check where AI is strictly banned (e.g., timed assessments or specific reflective essays).
  2. Declaration rules: Look for required disclosure forms, prompt log templates, or declaration statements.
  3. Authorship definitions: Ensure you understand what constitutes unacceptable automated text generation.
  4. Approved software: Identify whether specific institutional tools (e.g., university-licensed AI) are mandated.
  5. Support contacts: Locate departmental contacts for academic integrity inquiries.
  6. Module priority: Confirm that specific module guidelines match overarching university policies.

University learning support services in the UK offer extensive resources on referencing, proper research methods, and documenting AI usage. Developing strong habits in research and traditional assignment writing and essay writing ensure that your submitted work remains original and fully compliant with ethical standards.
